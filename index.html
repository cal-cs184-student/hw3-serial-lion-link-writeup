Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

For ray generation, we first convert the hFov and vFov variables to radians, then we convert to the image coordinates to sensor coordinates using the expressions given in the spec. We also need to make sure the previous origin at the bottom left is now the center in the camera space. Then we set the direction to point down the Z axis and transform from camera space to world space and normalize the ray. In the triangle intersection section, we used the Moller Trumbore algorithm from lecture. Since we have three unknowns (t and two of the barycentric coordinate weights for the triangle) and 3 axes, we can make a system of linear equations. The algorithm uses Cramer's Rule to find the solutions, but we also used the lecture slide as a guide to set the right components.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

For the BVH construction, we first reuse the root bounding box construction from the starter code while also keeping track of the number of primitices we encounter. Then, we add the base case where we compare the number of primitives to the maximum count for leaves. Otherwise, we choose the split point to be the average centroid along the longest axis of the centroids' bounding box, and partition the primitives to the left and right children based on which side of this point their centroid falls on. We chose the longest axis because that is where the primitives are most spread out.

Without the BVH, every ray is tested against every primitive, so the cost per ray is huge for big scenes. The average number of intersection tests per ray went from about 510 on the teapot (2,464 primitives) to 1,080 on the cow (5,856 primitices) and 5,761 on the bunny (28,588 primitives). Speed falls also, from 0.20 million rays per second on the teapot to 0.013 on the bunny. With the BVH, speed stayed between about 1.5 and 6 million rays per second whatever the scene size. That's 26x faster on the teapot, 17x on the cow, and about 470x on the bunny.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

We implemented two direct lighting functions. They estimate the reflection equation at a hit point by Monte Carlo sampling, which is L_out(p, ω_o), as an integral over incoming directions of f(ω_i -> ω_o) * L_i(p, ω_i) * cos θ_i. They differ in how directions are chosen.
Uniform hemisphere sampling samples diections uniformly over the hemisphere and cast a ray from the hit point along each one. If the ray hits a source of light, it gets an emission. Everything else contributes nothing. Each sample adds f * L_i * cos θ / (1/2*pi), and we average over all samples. The result is noisy, since most rays miss the light, and it can only capture light areas, not points.
Importance sampling samples the lights directly, instead of the entire hemisphere. sample_L returns the direction of each sampled point, its distance, emitted radiance, and the sample's pdf. We skip samples where the light is behind the surface. For each remaining sample we cast a ray toward the light. If nothing blocks it, the light reaches the poitn and we add its contribution f * L_i * cosθ / pdf . Each light's contribution si the average of its samples.

Comparison: At the same sample count, light sampling produces a much cleaner image than uniform hemisphere sampling. This happens because hemisphere sampling picks incoming directions without regard where the light is, so most rays miss the area light and contribute nothing. Only a few samples per pixel carry light, so the image is grainy, especially in soft shadows. Importance sampling aims at the light, so the same sampling gives a much smoother image since every sample contributes and variance drops significantly. Both methods should eventually give the same result, but light sampling gets there with far fewer samples. Additionally, light sampling also handles point lights, which hemisphere sampling cannot render, since a random direction has zero probability of hitting a single point.

With only one sample per pixel, the soft shadows depend entirely on how many shadow rays each pixel sends to the area light. At one light ray, the image is very noisy, and the soft shadow under the bunny is basically just random pixel noise. There is no visible shadow gradient. Under four light rays, the walls and the floor look better, and the shadow begins to form a clearer dark region, but there is still no gradient on the soft shadow. At sixteen light rays, the shape and gradient of the shadow becomes more noticeable but still grainy. Finally, at sixty four light rays, the shadow is a smooth gradient. Noise falls significantly.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Global illumination is computed recursively using the function at_least_one_bounce_radiance, which follows each light path backward from the camera, one bounce on each call. The camera rays start with depth max_ray_depth, and for each surface's emission, we use the est_radiance_global_illumination fucntion. At each hit point, we add the direct lighting from one_bounce_radiance. When isAccumBounces is false, we only add the last bounce, so the output isolates light that bounced exactly max_ray_depth times. Next, continue on a random direction, favoring directions close to the surface normal, and shoot a new ray that way with one less bounce remaining. If that ray hits something, call at_least_one_bounce_radiance again to find how much light comes back from there. We add that light, scaled by the surface's reflectance and the angle. With Russian roulette, each bounce continues with only a 65% chance. When it does continue, we divide by 0.65, so on average the result is the same.

With global illumination, the ceiling is lit by bounding of the floor, and the shadows are softer. We can see the blue and the red color reflected onto the sides of the objects.

The scene with direct only illumination contains only light that travels from the area light to a surface and then to the camera. Surfaces facing the lihgt are brighly lit, but the ceiling and the shadows are completely black, which doesn't look realistic. The indirect only illumation shows the opposite. It is dimmer overall, but those regions exactly are filled. Also, the red and blue walls tint the floor and the sides of the bunny. Adding the two together gives the full global illumination image, where indirect light softens shadows and adds the color bleeding that makes the scene look realistic.

m=0 shows only the light and m=1 adds direct lighting, leaving the ceiling and shadows black, similar to what we saw on the previous images. The 2nd bounce is dimmer and lights exactly the missing regions: the ceiling, the shadowed side of the bunny, and the shadows themselves. It also carries blue and brown reflections from the walls. The 3rd bounce is a faint version of the bunny, but you can't really distinguish the shadows. m=4 and m=5 are even more faint, almost black.
Compared to a rasterizer, which computes only the direct lighting and fakes the rest with ambient terms, the 2nd and 3rd bounces are the physically correct version of that ambient light. They soften the shadows, light surfaces facing away from the light, and add the color from other surfaces. These effects are what makes these images look realistic. A rasterizer cannot produce this.
Compared to unaccumulated, accumulated renders sum the individual bounces. The jump from m = 1 to m = 2 is the largest and past m = 3 the image barely changes, so most of the global illumination effect comes from the first few bounces.

These last images show the effect of russian roulette rendering: After the fourth bounce most of the rays have not continued bouncing, so there is little difference between m=4 and m=100.

At 1 sample per pixel the image almost only noise. Noise drops steadily as the sample count increases, roughly decresing with square root of the number of samples. By 16–64 samples the scene is clear but still somewhat grainy in the shadows and on the ceiling. At 1024 samples it is smooth.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Adaptive sampling allocates more samples to complex or noisy parts of an image, and stops sampling earlier for more simple parts of the image. The modifications we made to raytracing from Part 1.2 were fairly minimal. We just added the illuminance summations during the for loop that we already had for non-adaptive sampling, then check at samplesPerBatch whether I is less than the max tolerance * mu. We calculate mu and sigma from the expressions given in the spec. We also keep track of the number of samples we used so that we can update the sampleCountBuffer at the end.
